Flood Zones & Flood Insurance
What Washington Home Buyers Need to Know

Shi Hao Liu
Licensed WA Real Estate Broker ・ Kelly Right Real Estate ・ Updated October 23, 2026

You only have to buy flood insurance if your lender requires it — and your lender only requires it if the home sits in a FEMA-designated Special Flood Hazard Area. That determination comes from FEMA's flood maps, not from the city the home is in, so two houses a few blocks apart in Kirkland or Renton can land on opposite sides of the line. Most Eastside and King County homes are outside any mapped flood zone, but lowland areas near the Sammamish River, Issaquah Creek, Lake Sammamish, and a handful of other watersheds aren't. Here's how the zone, the insurance, and Washington's disclosure rules actually work.

What a "flood zone" actually means

FEMA maintains Flood Insurance Rate Maps (FIRMs) for most of the country, including King County, that divide land into risk zones. The one that matters most is the Special Flood Hazard Area (SFHA) — commonly called the 100-year floodplain — shown on the map as a zone beginning with the letter "A" or "V." Outside the SFHA, most land falls into Zone X, which generally indicates minimal flood hazard. "100-year floodplain" is a statistical label, not a prediction: it means the area has roughly a 1% chance of flooding in any given year, which adds up to a meaningfully higher lifetime chance over the life of a 30-year mortgage.

You can check any specific address yourself at FEMA's Flood Map Service Center (msc.fema.gov) before you write an offer. Your lender will run its own Standard Flood Hazard Determination during underwriting regardless, so it's worth knowing the answer early rather than finding out midway through financing.

When flood insurance is actually required

There's no Washington state law forcing a buyer to carry flood insurance. The requirement comes from federal law instead: under the Flood Disaster Protection Act, any federally regulated or federally insured lender — which covers virtually every bank and credit union, plus any loan sold on to Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac — must require flood insurance as a condition of closing a loan secured by a building in a Special Flood Hazard Area, and must keep requiring it for as long as the loan exists. If your home isn't in a mapped SFHA, your lender generally has no legal basis to require it, though nothing stops you from buying coverage voluntarily if you want the protection anyway.

Practically, this means the flood zone question tends to surface during your financing contingency, not your inspection contingency — your lender's underwriter flags it, not your home inspector. If a required policy can't be arranged in time, it can hold up your closing date, so it's one more reason to get your loan application moving early. What a flood insurance policy actually covers

Most flood coverage in the U.S. is written through the National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP), a federal program administered through participating private insurers. For a single-family home, an NFIP policy currently caps building coverage at $250,000 and contents coverage at $100,000 — two separate coverages you have to buy separately, since insuring the structure doesn't automatically protect what's inside it. Contents are settled at actual cash value (replacement cost minus depreciation), not full replacement cost, while the building itself can be settled at replacement cost if it's your primary residence and insured to at least 80% of its replacement value.

If a home's value exceeds the NFIP caps, or you simply want broader terms, private flood insurance is a legal alternative that many lenders now accept to satisfy the mandatory purchase requirement — worth asking your insurance agent or lender about if the NFIP limits feel thin relative to your purchase price.

One timing detail worth knowing: new NFIP policies typically carry a standard waiting period before coverage takes effect. That waiting period doesn't apply, however, when the policy is being purchased in connection with closing, increasing, extending, or renewing a loan — which is exactly the situation most home buyers are in, so coverage required for your purchase can generally take effect at closing rather than being delayed.

What it costs

FEMA moved to a new pricing methodology called Risk Rating 2.0, which prices each property individually based on its own specific flood risk — distance to water, elevation, flood history, and rebuilding cost — rather than relying mainly on which flood zone it sits in. That means two homes in the same neighborhood, even the same flood zone, can carry meaningfully different premiums. There's no reliable single "typical cost" figure to quote, since the range is wide and changes with FEMA's rate updates — the only way to know what a specific property will cost to insure is to get a quote from an NFIP-participating insurer or agent, ideally before your financing contingency deadline.

Where flood risk actually shows up on the Eastside and in King County

Flood risk in King County isn't evenly distributed, and it isn't simply a matter of which city a home is in. King County's Flood Control Zone District, a countywide special-purpose district created in 2007, manages flood-reduction projects along six major rivers: the Cedar, Sammamish, Green, White, Snoqualmie, and South Fork Skykomish. FEMA's own flood insurance study for King County covers lowland areas near the Sammamish River (running through the Woodinville–Bothell–Redmond corridor), Issaquah Creek, and the Lake Sammamish shoreline, among other spots across the county.

That doesn't mean homes in those cities are generally at flood risk — most aren't — but it does mean location within a city, not just the city itself, drives the answer. A home a short distance from Lake Washington, Lake Sammamish, or one of these river corridors deserves a specific flood map check, even if the listing agent hasn't mentioned it. If you're evaluating homes in Kenmore, Mercer Island, Issaquah, or Sammamish — all near significant water — this is worth a few minutes on FEMA's map tool before you get too attached to a specific property.

What Washington's seller disclosure says about flooding

Washington requires sellers of most residential property to complete a seller disclosure statement under RCW 64.06 — the form real estate professionals usually call "Form 17." Among its many questions, it asks the seller to disclose their actual knowledge of whether the property is located in a flood zone or floodway and whether it has any history of flooding. Like the rest of the form, this reflects only what the seller actually knows, not an independent inspection or survey — a seller who has never experienced flooding but whose home sits inside a mapped SFHA can answer honestly and still leave you needing to check FEMA's map yourself. Treat Form 17 as a starting point, not a substitute for verifying the zone directly.
