What Happens at Closing in Washington
A Buyer's Step-by-Step Timeline

Shi Hao Liu
Licensed WA Real Estate Broker ・ Kelly Right Real Estate ・ Updated November 19, 2026

Closing day in Washington isn't one single moment — it's a short sequence: you sign at an escrow or title company, your lender funds the loan, the deed gets recorded with the county auditor, and only then do you get your keys. Because Washington is a "dry-funding" state, signing and the official closing date often aren't the same day — most buyers sign one to three business days ahead of time. The sale isn't legally final, and ownership doesn't transfer, until the county auditor records the deed. Here's how each step actually works and what can slow it down.

Washington closes through escrow, not a table signing

Washington is an escrow-closing state. Instead of buyer, seller, and both agents sitting around a table for a single simultaneous signing (common in some East Coast "attorney states"), a neutral escrow agent — typically inside a title company — collects signed documents and funds from each side separately, then releases everything only once the whole file balances. Buyer and seller often sign on different days, sometimes without ever being in the same room.

Attorneys can be involved, but Washington doesn't require one to close a residential purchase the way some states do. Your escrow officer is the neutral party making sure money and documents move in the right order.

The core sequence: sign → fund → record → keys

Four things have to happen, in order, before you own the home:

  • Sign. You sign your closing package — the deed of trust, promissory note, settlement statement, and related documents — at the escrow or title company (or remotely, in some cases; more on that below). This commonly happens a day or more before the closing date written in your contract.
  • Fund. After reviewing your signed package, your lender wires the loan proceeds to escrow. Washington is widely classified as a "dry-funding" state (along with states like California, Oregon, Idaho, and Nevada), meaning funding doesn't have to happen on the same day as signing — it's common for the lender's wire to arrive one or two business days later, which is also typically when escrow requires your own down payment and closing-cost funds to already be in as collected funds.
  • Record. Once escrow confirms both your funds and the lender's wire have arrived and the file balances, it submits the statutory warranty deed (and deed of trust, if you financed) to the county auditor for recording, along with a completed Real Estate Excise Tax Affidavit. Washington law doesn't let the auditor record the deed until the real estate excise tax is paid or the transfer is confirmed exempt — see our guide to REET for how that tax is calculated.
  • Disburse and release keys. Once recording is confirmed, escrow disburses funds to the seller, lender, and any other payees, and the transaction is officially closed.

Why recording is the real finish line — and why cutoff times matter

Recording is what legally transfers title, so the exact minute it happens matters more than when you signed. County auditors only record during business hours, and each county sets its own daily cutoff. King County's Recorder's Office records documents Monday through Friday, 8:30 a.m. to 3:30 p.m.; anything submitted after 3:30 p.m. is recorded the next business day. If your lender's wire arrives late in the afternoon, or there's a problem with the excise tax affidavit, your "closing date" can effectively slip to the following business day even though you signed on schedule.

This is also why closing on a Friday — or right before a holiday weekend — carries slightly more risk: there's no same-day recovery window if a wire or document issue pops up late in the day.

When you actually get the keys

Under the standard NWMLS purchase and sale agreement, the seller is required to deliver keys, garage remotes, and any access codes on the Closing Date or the Possession Date, whichever is earlier, and the buyer is entitled to possession at 9:00 p.m. on the possession date unless the contract specifies otherwise. In practice, that almost always means after your escrow officer confirms the deed has recorded — not at your signing appointment, and not necessarily the moment you leave the title company's office.

If the seller needs a few extra days to move out after closing, or you want to move in before closing funds, that's handled through a separate rent-back or early-occupancy agreement (NWMLS Form 65B for seller occupancy after closing, or Form 65A for buyer occupancy before closing) rather than the default possession language — and it comes with its own per-day rent and insurance considerations worth negotiating up front, not after you're already under contract.

The final walkthrough

Most buyers schedule a final walkthrough of the home shortly before closing — commonly in the 24-to-48-hour window beforehand, though your agent can arrange it earlier if the seller has already moved out. It isn't a second inspection; it's a quick confirmation that the home is in the same condition as when you made your offer (or in the agreed condition, if you negotiated repairs through your inspection contingency), that included appliances and fixtures are still there, and that nothing was damaged during the seller's move-out. If something's wrong, it's far easier to resolve before you sign than after you've recorded and the seller has your money.

What can delay closing

A handful of things commonly push a closing date back in Washington:

  • Lender funding after the recording cutoff. Late underwriting conditions or a late wire can mean the county auditor's doors have already closed for the day.
  • Excise tax affidavit problems. The county can't record without a completed REET affidavit and payment (or a confirmed exemption).
  • Wire issues. A mismatched account number or a bank's internal delay can hold up the funds escrow needs to balance the file.
  • Last-minute title issues. An unresolved lien or a question raised by the title search can require a few extra days to clear.

Building a day or two of cushion into your moving-truck reservation, and being quick to return documents your lender or escrow officer requests in the final week, meaningfully lowers the odds of a last-minute scramble.

Signing remotely

Washington has allowed remote online notarization since 2020, and a 2026 update (House Bill 2158, signed March 12, 2026) extended that framework to cover remote notarization of paper documents as well, giving escrow companies more flexibility for buyers or sellers who can't make it to the title office in person — useful for an out-of-state buyer or a seller who has already relocated. Not every closing is eligible for remote signing, and requirements can vary by lender and title company, so ask your escrow officer early if you'll need that option.

Putting it together: a typical timeline

For a financed purchase, expect something close to this: mutual acceptance, then roughly 30-45 days of inspection, appraisal, and loan underwriting (nationally, ICE Mortgage Technology data put the average time to close a purchase loan at around 37-44 days through late 2025 and early 2026). In the final days, you'll sign your closing package one to three business days before the contract's closing date, your lender funds shortly after, escrow records the deed (subject to the county's daily cutoff), and you get your keys once recording is confirmed — often the same day as recording, sometimes the next business day. An all-cash purchase can compress this dramatically, sometimes closing within a week or two of an accepted offer, since there's no lender funding step to wait on.
