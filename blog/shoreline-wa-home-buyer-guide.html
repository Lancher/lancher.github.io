Buying a Home in Shoreline, WA
A 2026 Buyer's Guide

Shi Hao Liu
Licensed WA Real Estate Broker ・ Kelly Right Real Estate ・ Updated November 1, 2026

Shoreline sits directly on Seattle's northern border, and since August 2024 it's had its own stop on Sound Transit's 1 Line — two of them, in fact. Zillow put the typical Shoreline home value at about $785,500 as of August 2026, down roughly 2.4% from a year earlier, while Redfin's actual median sale price has run a bit lower depending on the month. The city is split by I-5 into two high school attendance zones, and both the new light rail stations have triggered years of rezoning and apartment construction around them. Here's what actually matters if you're considering a purchase here.

Where Shoreline fits

Shoreline sits immediately north of Seattle, bordering the city along North 145th Street, and extends north to roughly North 205th Street at the King-Snohomish county line, where it borders the town of Woodway and unincorporated Snohomish County. The city incorporated on August 31, 1995, carved out of what had been unincorporated King County, and its population was 58,608 at the 2020 Census. Shoreline is entirely within King County — unlike Bothell to the east, there's no county line to track here. The city splits across three ZIP codes: 98133 in the southeast (shared with north Seattle), 98155 in the northeast, and 98177 in the west, which covers the Richmond Beach area along Puget Sound.

Getting around: two new light rail stations, plus I-5 and Aurora

The biggest recent change in Shoreline is transportation. Shoreline North/185th Station and Shoreline South/148th Station both opened on August 30, 2024, as part of Sound Transit's Lynnwood Link Extension, giving the city its first-ever rail stops on the 1 Line. Both stations sit along I-5 — 148th Street's station is elevated with a roughly 500-stall parking garage, while 185th Street's station also connects to Community Transit's Swift Blue Line bus rapid transit. For drivers, I-5 runs the length of the city and is the fastest way south into Seattle or north toward Everett, while Aurora Avenue N (SR-99) is the other major north-south corridor, lined with retail and increasingly with new apartment buildings. The Interurban Trail, a paved multi-use path completed in 2008 along the route of an early-1900s electric railway, also runs the length of the city near Aurora and connects to trail networks in both Seattle and Snohomish County.

One city, two high schools split by I-5

Shoreline is served by the Shoreline School District, and unlike most Eastside cities covered in this blog, the district's attendance zones split the city itself rather than following city limits. West of I-5, students feed into Shorewood High School and Einstein Middle School, with elementary feeders including Echo Lake, Highland Terrace, Meridian Park, Parkwood, and Syre. East of I-5, students feed into Shorecrest High School and Kellogg Middle School, with elementary feeders including Briarcrest, Brookside, and Ridgecrest. Both high schools are generally well regarded, and their relative rankings shift depending on the source and the year you check, so don't assume one side of the freeway is objectively "better" — confirm the exact attendance zone for any address you're considering directly with the district, since boundaries don't always follow the streets you'd expect.

Shoreline's neighborhoods

Shoreline's housing stock varies a lot by area — waterfront streets near Puget Sound, mid-century rambler neighborhoods built around their local elementary schools, and newer mixed-use development near the light rail stations:

  • Richmond Beach — the city's Puget Sound waterfront neighborhood in the far northwest corner (ZIP 98177), anchored by Richmond Beach Saltwater Park and a small historic business district, with homes ranging from modest older cottages to larger view properties on the bluffs above the beach.
  • Ridgecrest — an east-side, Shorecrest-zoned neighborhood of mostly single-family homes on quiet residential streets.
  • Echo Lake — a west-side, Shorewood-zoned neighborhood near the small lake of the same name, close to Aurora Avenue and the 185th Street light rail station area.
  • North City — a walkable commercial and residential node around NE 175th Street and 15th Avenue NE, with small shops, restaurants, and a mix of older and newer housing.
  • Ballinger — a neighborhood near Lake Ballinger on the city's northeast edge, bordering Mountlake Terrace.

Because several of these neighborhood names match the elementary school that serves them, it's worth double-checking the specific attendance zone for a given address — ask your agent to pull the exact school assignment, not just the neighborhood name, before you get attached to a listing.

What homes cost in Shoreline in 2026

Shoreline's price data varies more than a larger city's because sales volume each month is relatively small. Zillow's typical home value estimate was about $785,500 as of August 2026, down roughly 2.4% year-over-year. Redfin's median sale price — the actual median of homes that closed, rather than a smoothed estimate — has run lower and swung more by month: around $742,500 in a recent November (down 4.8% year-over-year on 42 sales), and closer to $770,000 across roughly 288 closings over a trailing six-month window reported in September 2026. Local monthly market reports have generally described Shoreline as a seller's market through 2026, with homes selling in a median of roughly 19 to 22 days in the spring of that year and inventory running under two months of supply. As with any smaller city, a handful of waterfront or fixer-upper sales can swing a given month's median noticeably — ask your agent for comparables specific to the neighborhood, lot type, and condition you're looking at.

What's changing in Shoreline

The city rezoned the areas around both future light rail stations years before they opened — the 185th Street station area in 2015 and the 148th Street station area in 2016 — allowing taller, denser apartment buildings near each stop. That rezoning has already brought thousands of new apartment units either built or in the pipeline around the 148th Street station in particular, with more gradual change so far around 185th Street, where part of the zoning doesn't fully unlock until 2033. For a buyer, that means parts of Shoreline near the two stations will keep adding new multifamily housing and retail for years, which can bring more walkable amenities but also ongoing construction and traffic in those specific pockets — worth asking about if you're looking at a single-family home within a few blocks of either station.

What to watch for as a buyer

  • Older housing stock. Much of Shoreline's single-family inventory dates to the 1940s through 1960s, with the same kinds of issues that show up across the region's older homes — aging wiring, outdated plumbing, and roofs or foundations due for attention. Our common inspection findings in Pacific Northwest homes guide covers what to expect, and our home inspection contingency guide covers how to structure that protection in your offer.
  • Competitive offers on well-priced listings. With a median of around three weeks on market in a seller's market, a well-priced Shoreline home can draw multiple offers quickly. See our guide on how to win a bidding war in Seattle & the Eastside.
  • Sewer and older pipe in some neighborhoods. Homes built before the 1970s in any Puget Sound-area city can still have clay or Orangeburg sewer laterals. Our sewer scope inspection guide explains what a scope checks and what it costs.
  • Confirm the attendance zone, not just the neighborhood. Because Shoreline's high school boundary runs along I-5 rather than following neighborhood lines, two homes a few blocks apart can feed into different middle and high schools.
