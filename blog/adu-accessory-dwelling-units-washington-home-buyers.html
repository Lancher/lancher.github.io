Accessory Dwelling Units (ADUs)
What Washington Home Buyers Need to Know

Shi Hao Liu
Licensed WA Real Estate Broker ・ Kelly Right Real Estate ・ Updated November 7, 2026

Since 2023, Washington state law has required nearly every city and county — including every Eastside city — to allow up to two accessory dwelling units on most lots zoned for a single-family home, whether attached, detached, or one of each. Cities can no longer require the owner to live on the property, and they can't impose off-street parking requirements near major transit. That means the basement apartment, backyard cottage, or future mother-in-law suite you're picturing is very likely legal to build — but local lot size rules, permitting, financing, and even your future HOA's own covenants can still change what's actually possible on a specific property. Here's how the law works and what to check before you count on an ADU.

What counts as an ADU

An accessory dwelling unit is a second, complete living space on a lot that already has (or will have) a primary single-family home. It has its own kitchen, bathroom, and sleeping area and is legally and physically separate from the main house. Washington recognizes two basic types: an attached ADU (sometimes called an AADU), built inside or added onto the main house — a basement apartment or a unit over a garage — and a detached ADU (a DADU), a freestanding backyard cottage. A finished basement with a bedroom and bathroom isn't automatically an ADU; it only counts once it's a self-contained unit with its own kitchen and is permitted as a separate dwelling.

Washington's 2023 law: up to two ADUs per lot, statewide

House Bill 1337, passed by the legislature and signed into law in 2023, is now codified mainly at RCW 36.70A.680 and RCW 36.70A.681. It applies to every city and county that plans under Washington's Growth Management Act — which covers King County and every Eastside city, including Redmond, Bellevue, Kirkland, Sammamish, Issaquah, Woodinville, Kenmore, Bothell, Renton, Newcastle, and Mercer Island. Within urban growth areas, these jurisdictions must now allow, on any lot large enough for a single-family home:

  • Up to two ADUs per lot — two attached units, one attached and one detached, or two detached units (cities may limit this to a combined total of two units, including the main house, on lots of 2,000 square feet or smaller).
  • No owner-occupancy requirement. Cities can't require you to live in the main house (or the ADU) as a condition of renting either one out.
  • Limited parking requirements. Cities generally can't require off-street parking for an ADU on a lot within roughly a half mile of a major transit stop, and are otherwise capped at one or two spaces per unit depending on lot size.
  • Capped impact fees. Any impact fee charged for an ADU can't exceed 50% of the fee charged for the principal home on the same lot.

Cities in the first tier of the state's periodic comprehensive plan review had to bring their local development regulations into compliance by June 30, 2025; others follow on their own update schedule. Where a local code still conflicts with the state standard, the state law controls — a city's older, stricter ordinance is preempted rather than enforceable.

Your specific city's rules still matter

The state law sets a floor, not a single uniform code. Maximum ADU size, height, setbacks, design standards, and exactly how parking and lot-size exceptions apply still vary from one Eastside city to the next, and some cities finished updating their codes well before others. Before assuming a property can support an ADU of a particular size, check the current zoning code and talk to the city's planning or permitting department directly — Redmond, Bellevue, and Kirkland each publish their ADU standards online, and staff can confirm what a specific lot allows. For a broader look at what makes each city distinct, see our guides to Redmond, Bellevue, and Kirkland.

Buying a home that already has an ADU — permitted or not

Plenty of older Eastside and King County homes already have a basement apartment, a backyard cottage, or a mother-in-law suite that was never permitted as a legal ADU. This is one of the more common issues that surfaces during a home sale, alongside the kinds of aging-systems findings covered in our guide to common inspection findings in Pacific Northwest homes. An unpermitted unit isn't automatically a dealbreaker, but it carries real risk:

  • It may not be on the tax rolls as a legal unit, which can affect how a lender and appraiser value the property.
  • A lender typically won't count rental income from an unpermitted unit toward loan qualifying.
  • The seller's Form 17 disclosure should note whether any addition or conversion was done without permits — ask your agent to flag this specifically if it isn't addressed.
  • Retroactive permitting is sometimes possible but can require bringing the space up to current code, which may cost more than you'd expect.

If a listing's basement or detached structure is marketed as rental-ready, ask for the permit record (most cities post permit history online) before you factor that income into your offer.

Can you finance a home purchase using ADU rental income?

Sometimes, but it depends on the loan program and whether the unit is legal. Freddie Mac's published guidelines let a buyer purchasing a one-unit primary residence with an ADU count a portion of its rental income toward qualifying — generally 75% of the lesser of the appraiser's market rent opinion or an existing lease, with ADU income capped at 30% of total qualifying income, and an appraisal that specifically addresses the ADU is required. Fannie Mae, FHA, and individual lenders set their own rules, and those rules have been changing as ADUs become more common, so don't assume a specific number without confirming it with your loan officer during pre-approval. See our mortgage pre-approval guide for what lenders check more generally.

HOA and condo covenants can still say no

State and city zoning law is only half the picture. A homeowners association's CC&Rs are a private contract, and plenty of HOA-governed Eastside subdivisions — common in cities like Sammamish and Issaquah — restrict or ban detached structures, additional dwelling units, or rentals altogether, regardless of what the city's zoning code now permits. Before you buy with an ADU in mind, read the HOA's governing documents, not just the city code. Our guide to reviewing HOA and condo documents walks through what to request and how to read it.

Selling or buying a home with a separately-sold ADU

Washington's 2023 law also addresses whether an ADU can be sold separately from the main house. Where a legal process exists — typically a condominium conversion or a unit lot subdivision — a city can't block the sale solely because the unit started out as an "accessory" structure. In practice, that still requires separating the ADU's utility connections from the main house and completing your city's condo or subdivision process, so it's a meaningfully bigger step than simply listing a room for rent. If you're looking at a listing marketed as a house with a separately-titled ADU, or considering creating one yourself, loop in a real estate attorney and the city's planning department early — this is still a newer process in most Eastside jurisdictions.

Budgeting for utility and permit costs

Beyond design and construction, King County charges its own wastewater capacity charge when a property connects to the county sewer system, billed either as a lump sum at permit issuance or spread over 15 years with interest; King County's current rate is $77.99 per month per residential customer equivalent (RCE) for connections made in 2026, with the RCE factor for a given unit set by King County's wastewater rate schedule. That charge is separate from the permit and construction costs charged by your city, and it's easy to leave out of an early ADU budget — ask King County Wastewater Treatment Division or your contractor how it applies to your specific unit before you commit to a number.

Weigh an ADU into your home search early

If future rental income, aging parents, or a grown child moving back home is part of why you're buying on the Eastside, it's worth raising with your buyer's agent before you write an offer — not after you own the home. Lot size, slope, septic or well capacity (see our guide to buying on septic or well in King County), tree canopy rules, and your future HOA's covenants can all affect whether a specific property can realistically support a second unit, even where the city's zoning code says yes.

General information, not legal, financial, or zoning advice. Washington's ADU statute (RCW 36.70A.680, .681) sets statewide minimum standards; individual city and county codes, lot-size thresholds, and compliance timelines vary and change over time. Mortgage rental-income guidelines are set by Freddie Mac, Fannie Mae, FHA, and individual lenders and are subject to change. Confirm current zoning rules with your city's planning department, current loan-qualifying rules with your lender, and HOA restrictions by reading the association's recorded CC&Rs before relying on any of the figures above.
