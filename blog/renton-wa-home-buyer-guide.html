Buying a Home in Renton, WA
A 2026 Buyer's Guide

Shi Hao Liu
Licensed WA Real Estate Broker ・ Kelly Right Real Estate ・ Updated September 23, 2026

Renton sits at the south end of Lake Washington, on I-405 and SR-167, just south of Bellevue — and it's the most affordable city of its size near the Eastside job corridor. Redfin put Renton's citywide median sale price at roughly $670,000 in June 2026, and Zillow's typical-home-value estimate has run somewhat higher, around $720,000 — both well below King County's overall median of about $890,000, and far below Bellevue ($1.5M), Kirkland ($1.4M), or Redmond ($1.3M). It's home to Boeing's 737 assembly plant, IKEA's first Pacific Northwest store, and a genuinely diverse mix of neighborhoods, from lakefront Kennydale to the Renton Highlands plateau. Here's what actually matters if you're considering a purchase here.

Where Renton fits

Renton incorporated on September 6, 1901, making it one of the older cities in King County. It covers roughly 23.5 square miles of land at the southern tip of Lake Washington, bordered by Bellevue and Newcastle to the north, Kent to the south, and unincorporated King County — including Fairwood and the separate community of Skyway — to the east and west. The 2020 Census counted 106,785 residents, and more recent estimates put the population a little higher. Renton sits directly on I-405 and SR-167, which makes it one of the more freeway-accessible cities in the region, even though it's usually counted as its own South King County city rather than part of the Eastside proper.

The most affordable city near the Eastside job corridor

Renton to downtown Bellevue via I-405 is about 12 miles, typically a 20-25 minute drive outside of peak hours — closer than Issaquah in travel time, and directly on the freeway the whole way. Downtown Seattle is a similar distance via I-405 and I-5, generally 20-30 minutes off-peak. Kent, SeaTac Airport, and the rest of South King County are all a short drive south on SR-167 or I-405. For buyers weighing the Eastside's high prices against a shorter budget, Renton is worth cross-shopping against Redmond and Bellevue for the price difference, keeping in mind that peak-hour I-405 traffic can be heavy in both directions.

Mostly one school district — with real exceptions

Most of the city falls within Renton School District No. 403, which enrolled roughly 13,400 students in fall 2025 across elementary, middle, and high schools including Renton, Lindbergh, and Hazen High. The district's boundaries extend well beyond city limits into parts of Newcastle, Bellevue, Kent, Tukwila, and unincorporated King County, including the West Hill area near Skyway. But it isn't the only district touching Renton: parts of the Renton-area community of Fairwood, despite carrying a Renton mailing address, fall within the Kent School District instead. As with any Eastside or South King County purchase, confirm the assigned elementary, middle, and high school for the specific address before writing an offer — district lines don't follow city limits or zip codes cleanly.

Renton's neighborhoods

Renton is large enough, and old enough, to have a genuinely varied set of neighborhoods rather than a single character:

  • Kennydale — a lakefront neighborhood north of downtown along Lake Washington, with a mix of older homes and newer construction, some with lake views or access.
  • Renton Highlands — a large residential plateau northeast of downtown, built out mostly after World War II with ongoing infill and newer development.
  • Talbot Hill — south of downtown, anchored by Valley Medical Center, with a mix of established single-family homes.
  • Benson Hill — a suburban single-family area in southeast Renton, largely developed from the 1960s onward.
  • Fairwood — a mixed incorporated/unincorporated area southeast of the city with its own golf course and shopping, partly zoned to Kent schools as noted above.
  • Downtown Renton — the historic core, the focus of the city's ongoing Civic Core redevelopment plan, and closest to the Renton Transit Center and The Landing.

West of downtown, Renton also borders Skyway — a distinct, unincorporated King County community that is not part of the city of Renton, despite the proximity and shared services in some areas. If a listing mentions Skyway, confirm whether the address is actually inside Renton city limits, since that affects which city services, taxes, and school assignment apply.

What homes cost in Renton in 2026

Renton's median prices sit well below the rest of the Eastside. Redfin's citywide median sale price ran around $670,000 as of June 2026, down roughly 7.6% from a year earlier, while Zillow's broader typical-home-value estimate for the city has run somewhat higher, closer to $720,000, also down over the past year. Both figures sit well under King County's overall median of about $890,000 in June 2026, and far under Redmond's $1.3 million or Bellevue's $1.5 million. That said, Renton's price range is wide — from smaller, older homes in some southern zip codes to newer construction near Renton Highlands and Fairwood pushing well past $900,000 — so a citywide median is only a starting point. Ask for comparables specific to the neighborhood and zip code you're considering.

Boeing, IKEA, and Renton's job base

Renton's economy is anchored by manufacturing in a way most Eastside cities aren't. Boeing's Renton plant, on the south shore of Lake Washington, assembles the 737 family of aircraft and remains one of the region's largest employers. IKEA opened its first Pacific Northwest store in Renton in 1994 and rebuilt it on the same site, reopening in 2017. PACCAR, parent of Kenworth Trucks, also has a long-standing presence in the city. Closer to downtown, The Landing — a roughly 68-acre mixed-use retail and entertainment district built on former Boeing land — anchors more than 500,000 square feet of shops, restaurants, and a movie theater, and Renton Technical College serves the area's workforce-training needs. For buyers, that mix means a more industrial and blue-collar job base alongside the newer retail and office development than you'll find in Bellevue or Redmond.

What's changing in Renton

The city has been working through a Downtown Civic Core Vision and Action Plan, adopted in 2018, aimed at adding public gathering space, streetscape improvements, and mixed-use development to the historic core — a long-term project still being implemented in phases. North of The Landing, the Southport development on the former Shuffleton power plant site is bringing lakefront office and hotel space to the waterfront. On transit, Sound Transit broke ground on a rebuilt Renton Transit Center in February 2026 as part of its I-405 Stride S1 bus rapid transit line, which will eventually connect Renton to Bellevue, Tukwila, Burien, and beyond — though that line isn't expected to open for riders until 2028. In the meantime, King County Metro's RapidRide F Line already connects the Renton Transit Center and The Landing to Tukwila and Burien.

What to watch for as a buyer

  • Competitive offers on well-priced listings. Renton's lower price point draws strong buyer demand, and desirable homes can still see multiple offers. Our guide on how to win a bidding war on the Eastside covers escalation clauses and contingency strategy that apply just as well here.
  • Which school district actually applies. With Renton and Kent school districts both serving parts of the same general area, confirm the exact attendance zone for any address before you fall in love with a listing.
  • Older housing stock in some neighborhoods. Renton's older neighborhoods can mean aging systems and deferred maintenance. Our home inspection contingency guide covers when a sewer scope or structural inspection is worth adding.
  • Flight noise and industrial proximity. Homes near Boeing's plant, the Renton Municipal Airport, or industrial areas along the Green River valley can carry more noise than a typical suburban listing — worth a visit at different times of day before you write an offer.
