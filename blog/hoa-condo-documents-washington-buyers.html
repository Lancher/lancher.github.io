HOA & Condo Documents in Washington
What to Review Before You Buy

Shi Hao Liu
Licensed WA Real Estate Broker ・ Kelly Right Real Estate ・ Updated September 19, 2026

Buying a condo or a home in a Washington HOA means buying into the association, not just the unit. State law requires the association to give you a resale certificate — its financials, reserves, rules, and any pending lawsuits — and once you receive it you get a 5-day right to cancel that can't be waived. The association can charge no more than $275 for it and generally must deliver it within 10 days of a request. Skip reading it carefully, and you can inherit an underfunded HOA's problems the day you close.

Why the paperwork matters as much as the home

A home can pass every part of a home inspection and still be a bad buy if the association behind it is in trouble. Underfunded reserves, a special assessment the board hasn't announced yet, an active lawsuit, or rental rules that block the plan you had for the unit — none of that shows up on a walkthrough. It shows up in the paperwork. Just as you'd never waive an inspection on an older home without understanding the risk, you shouldn't skip reading the HOA or condo documents on a shared-ownership property. Both are due diligence; they just look at different things.

Two laws, depending on when your building was formed

Washington condos and HOAs aren't all governed by the same statute yet. Condominiums created before July 1, 2018 are generally governed by the older Washington Condominium Act (RCW 64.34). Communities formed on or after that date fall under the newer Washington Uniform Common Interest Ownership Act, or WUCIOA (RCW 64.90), which also covers non-condo planned communities and co-ops with mandatory HOA dues. The two laws are similar on the points that matter most to buyers — resale certificates, disclosure, and your cancellation right — but knowing which one applies to your specific building tells you exactly which statute defines your rights. Your agent, escrow officer, or the association's governing documents can confirm which law applies.

What's actually in a resale certificate

The resale certificate is the single most useful document you'll get before buying into an association. It typically discloses:

  • Current dues and whether any are unpaid on the specific unit you're buying.
  • The reserve fund balance and, where one exists, the association's reserve study — a professional estimate of what major repairs (roofs, siding, elevators, parking structures) will cost and when.
  • Planned or discussed special assessments — extra one-time bills beyond regular dues.
  • Pending or threatened litigation involving the association.
  • Insurance coverage carried by the association (the "master policy").
  • Rules and restrictions on rentals, pets, parking, and use of common areas.
  • Copies of the governing documents — the declaration/CC&Rs, bylaws, and rules — plus recent board or annual meeting minutes.

It's dense reading, but it's also the closest thing to an inspection report for the organization you're joining, not just the walls around you.

The timeline that actually protects you: 10 days to deliver, 5 days to cancel

Once a resale certificate is requested, the association generally has up to 10 days to produce it, and can charge no more than $275 to do so (RCW 64.90.640; older condos follow a similar rule under RCW 64.34.425). Once you actually receive it, you have a 5-day right to cancel the purchase — a protection that cannot be waived, shortened, or negotiated away, similar in spirit to your earnest money protections under a financing or inspection contingency.

One nuance worth knowing: a 2025 amendment to the law lets a seller deliver the certificate before you've even signed a purchase agreement, and the 5-day clock now runs from when you receive it, not from when you sign. If a certificate arrives with the listing packet, don't assume you'll get a fresh five days once you're under contract — read it as soon as it lands, because the clock may already be running.

2026 is a turning point for every Washington HOA

WUCIOA is scheduled to become the single law for every common interest community in Washington — condos, HOAs, and co-ops alike — by January 1, 2028. A 2025 bill, ESSB 5129, moved several governance and transparency provisions up to January 1, 2026, applying them to every association in the state regardless of when it was formed. Among the changes: boards must set aside at least 15 minutes for homeowner comments at meetings, associations may hold meetings remotely, and any written materials given to board members ahead of a meeting must also be made available to owners. For buyers, that means even an older, previously exempt HOA is now operating under new transparency rules — a good reason to ask for recent meeting minutes and see whether the association is actually keeping up.

A buyer's checklist for the documents

  • Trend the dues. Look at increases over the last two to three years, not just the current number.
  • Compare reserves to the reserve study. A fund that's well below what the study recommends is a strong signal of a special assessment down the road.
  • Read the litigation section closely. Construction-defect and insurance disputes can drag on for years and affect financing.
  • Check rental and use restrictions if you might ever rent the unit out or run a home business.
  • Scan recent meeting minutes for recurring complaints — leaks, elevator outages, parking disputes — that hint at bigger issues.
  • Ask your lender about the association. Conventional, FHA, and VA loans all have eligibility rules tied to an association's reserves, owner-occupancy rate, and litigation history, which can affect your financing now and your buyer pool at resale.

Red flags that should make you slow down

  • Reserves funded well below the reserve study's recommended level.
  • A special assessment that's been discussed but not yet formally issued — often a surprise bill that lands right after buyers close.
  • Active or repeated litigation against the association.
  • A high rate of owners delinquent on dues.
  • Frequent turnover of the board or management company.

None of these automatically mean walk away — plenty of well-run associations are simply catching up on deferred maintenance. But they're exactly the kind of thing you want to know before you're financially tied to the outcome, not after.

Who orders it, who pays, and who should review it

The seller (through the association or its management company) typically requests and provides the resale certificate; the cost is often addressed in the purchase agreement and shows up alongside your other closing costs. Your agent should build time to actually read it into your offer's document-review or inspection period, the same way a competitive Eastside offer still needs a real inspection contingency. For anything that looks genuinely concerning — active litigation, a large gap in reserves — a real estate attorney's second opinion is worth the cost before you commit. Where this comes up most on the Eastside

Condos and townhomes make up a meaningful share of inventory in and around downtown Redmond, Bellevue, and Kirkland, especially for buyers prioritizing walkability and a shorter commute over a larger single-family lot. That's exactly where a careful read of the resale certificate pays off — a well-run association can make a smaller unit an easy, low-maintenance buy, while a struggling one can turn "no yard work" into "surprise five-figure assessment."

On a condo or townhome purchase, every dollar you don't spend on commission is a dollar available for move-in costs, dues, or your share of a future reserve contribution.
