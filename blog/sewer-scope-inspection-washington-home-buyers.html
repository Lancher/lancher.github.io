Sewer Scope Inspections
What Washington Home Buyers Need to Know

Shi Hao Liu
Licensed WA Real Estate Broker ・ Kelly Right Real Estate ・ Updated September 27, 2026

A sewer scope is a video camera inspection run through the home's sewer cleanout and down the side sewer line connecting the house to the public sewer main — the one part of the plumbing system a general home inspector can't see. It typically costs $200-$650 in the Seattle area, most commonly $250-$400 as a standalone service. In a region full of homes with aging clay or "Orangeburg" pipe and mature trees with aggressive roots, it's one of the highest-value few hundred dollars an Eastside or Seattle buyer can spend before closing. Here's what it checks, what it costs, who's responsible for the line, and how to order one under Washington's standard inspection contingency.

What a sewer scope actually checks

A general home inspection covers what's visible and accessible — roof, foundation, framing, electrical, plumbing fixtures, heating and cooling. It does not include the underground side sewer line running from the house to the public main, often 30 to 100+ feet away and several feet deep. A sewer scope closes that gap: a plumber or inspector feeds a waterproof camera through a cleanout or a toilet flange and records the full run of pipe, looking for cracks, offset or separated joints, bellied (sagging) sections that trap waste, root intrusion, and, in the worst cases, a fully collapsed section of pipe.

Why it matters so much in Seattle & the Eastside

Two things make this region's older housing stock particularly sewer-scope-worthy. First, pipe materials: Orangeburg pipe — a wood-pulp-and-coal-tar material used roughly from the 1940s through the 1970s — has an average lifespan of only about 30 to 50 years, and it absorbs moisture, softens, and gradually collapses under soil pressure over time. Pre-1960s clay tile pipe is built from short sections joined by mortar, and those joints are exactly where tree roots find their way in. Second, mature trees: much of the Eastside and Seattle's older neighborhoods — think established streets in Kirkland, Bellevue, Redmond, and the close-in Seattle neighborhoods — were built up and landscaped decades ago, and root intrusion through aging pipe joints is one of the most common findings inspectors report. If a home was built before the 1980s, there's a real chance it's still relying on one of these older materials somewhere along the line.

Who's actually responsible for the side sewer

This part surprises a lot of buyers: the public sewer main under the street belongs to the city, but the pipe running from the house out to that main — the side sewer — belongs to the property owner. In Seattle, this is spelled out in Seattle Municipal Code Chapter 21.16: the property owner is responsible for the side sewer from the building all the way to its connection with the public main, and that responsibility can extend past the property line, under the sidewalk or street. Some homes even share a side sewer with a neighboring property before it reaches the main, which means shared maintenance responsibility. Bellevue's municipal code (BCC 24.04.115 and 24.04.210) works the same way — the property owner owns and is financially responsible for the private side sewer, while the city's Wastewater Division maintains only the main line itself. Redmond and Kirkland structure their utility codes similarly. In plain terms: whoever owns the home on the day the sewer backs up owns the repair bill, which is exactly why you want to know the line's condition before you're that owner.

How to get one during a Washington home purchase

A sewer scope is not automatically part of a general home inspection here. Washington's standard inspection contingency, NWMLS Form 35, lists several types of inspection a buyer can select — general, sewer/septic, structural, pest — and a sewer scope has to be specifically checked on the form to be included. The buyer pays for it, and like the rest of your inspection, it's ordered and completed during the inspection contingency period, which in most Eastside offers runs somewhere around five to ten days after mutual acceptance. Many general home inspectors either offer a sewer scope as an add-on or can refer a plumber who specializes in it, so it's often scheduled for the same visit as your general inspection to save a trip.

What a scope might find, and what it costs to fix

If the scope turns up a problem, the price range is wide depending on severity:

  • Minor root intrusion can sometimes be cleared with a professional cleaning or hydro-jetting for a few hundred dollars, though roots often return without a longer-term fix.
  • Trenchless pipe lining (CIPP) — where a resin-coated liner is inserted and cured inside the existing pipe rather than digging it up — commonly runs somewhere in the range of $7,000-$21,000 for a typical Seattle-area side sewer, or roughly $80-$250 per linear foot, depending on length, depth, and access.
  • Full excavation and replacement can cost more once landscaping, driveway, or hardscape restoration is factored in; trenchless methods often finish meaningfully cheaper than full dig-and-replace once that restoration is included.
  • Permits add to the total — Seattle requires a side sewer permit for this kind of work, typically adding roughly $500-$1,500 or more depending on scope.

These are general ranges, not a quote — get an actual bid from a licensed plumber or trenchless contractor for any specific home before you rely on a number.

What to do if the scope finds a problem

Because the sewer scope happens during your active inspection contingency, a bad finding gives you real leverage, the same as any other defect uncovered during inspection: you can ask the seller to complete the repair before closing, negotiate a price reduction or closing credit to cover it yourself (typically documented on NWMLS Form 35R, the inspection response), or cancel the purchase and recover your earnest money if the problem is serious enough that you'd rather walk. What you don't want is to skip the scope, close on the home, and discover a collapsed 60-year-old clay pipe the first time it backs up — at that point, the repair is entirely your bill with no seller at the negotiating table.

Where this fits in your buying process

A sewer scope is one specialty inspection worth adding to your home inspection contingency alongside the general inspection — it's a small add-on cost relative to what it can catch. If it does turn up a repair, factor that number into the same budget conversation as your other closing costs, since a credit negotiated through inspection can offset it.
