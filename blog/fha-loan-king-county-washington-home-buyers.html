FHA Loans in King County, WA
What Home Buyers Need to Know

Shi Hao Liu
Licensed WA Real Estate Broker ・ Kelly Right Real Estate ・ Updated November 21, 2026

An FHA loan lets you buy a home with as little as 3.5% down and a credit score in the high 500s or low 600s — often the lowest barrier to entry of any mainstream mortgage type. In King County, the trade-off is mortgage insurance that typically sticks around for the life of the loan, a 2026 loan limit of $1,063,750, and extra scrutiny if you're buying a condo. Here's how FHA financing actually works for a King County or Eastside purchase, and where it fits (or doesn't) against conventional, VA, and jumbo loans.

What an FHA loan actually is

FHA loans are originated by ordinary mortgage lenders — banks, credit unions, and mortgage companies — but insured by the Federal Housing Administration, a part of the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD). Because the government insures the lender against loss if you default, lenders can approve FHA loans with lower credit scores and smaller down payments than most conventional programs allow. That insurance isn't free: you pay for it yourself, through mortgage insurance premiums covered below.

Credit score and down payment: the 580 line

FHA sets two tiers based on credit score:

  • 580 or higher: minimum down payment of 3.5% of the purchase price.
  • 500 to 579: minimum down payment of 10%.
  • Below 500: FHA won't insure the loan at all.

Those are HUD's published floors, not a guarantee that any given lender will approve you at those scores. Most lenders add their own "overlay" on top — a common one is a 620 minimum — because they carry some of the early-payment-default risk themselves. If your score sits in the 580-640 range, shop more than one lender; approval and pricing can vary meaningfully between them for exactly this reason.

King County's 2026 FHA loan limit: $1,063,750

FHA sets its own loan limits each year, county by county, and in high-cost areas that limit moves together with the conventional conforming loan limit. For 2026, the FHA limit for a one-unit home in King, Pierce, and Snohomish counties is $1,063,750 — the same figure that applies to conventional conforming loans in these counties, as covered in our jumbo loan guide. That's well above the FHA baseline limit that applies to most of the country, which reflects how expensive King County housing is relative to the national market.

Remember that this caps your loan amount, not your home's purchase price. A buyer putting 10% down can use FHA financing on a home priced close to $1.18 million before the loan itself crosses the limit; a buyer putting the minimum 3.5% down tops out a bit lower, since less of the purchase price is covered by the down payment.

Mortgage insurance: the real cost of a low down payment

FHA mortgage insurance comes in two pieces:

  • Upfront mortgage insurance premium (UFMIP): 1.75% of your base loan amount, due at closing. Almost every borrower rolls this into the loan balance rather than paying it in cash.
  • Annual mortgage insurance premium (MIP): for a standard 30-year loan with the minimum 3.5% down payment, this commonly runs 0.55% of the loan balance per year, collected as part of your monthly payment. The exact rate varies by your down payment, loan term, and loan amount, so confirm your specific rate with your lender.

How long you're stuck paying annual MIP depends on your down payment: put down 10% or more and it cancels after 11 years; put down less than that — including the standard 3.5% — and it generally runs for the full life of the loan, with no automatic cancellation at 20% equity the way conventional private mortgage insurance works. The only way out at that point is usually refinancing into a conventional loan once your equity and credit support it.

Seller concessions: a 6% cap

FHA allows the seller (or another interested party) to contribute up to 6% of the purchase price toward your closing costs, prepaid expenses, and discount points — generally more room than conventional loans with less than 10% down allow, and more than VA's 4% cap on VA-defined concessions. That money can't be used toward your required down payment, and if contributions exceed what your actual eligible costs add up to, the extra doesn't just disappear into your pocket — lenders apply it only to allowable costs. Buying a condo with FHA financing

This is where FHA gets more complicated than a typical single-family purchase. FHA will only insure a loan on a condo unit if the condominium project itself is FHA-approved, or if the specific unit qualifies under FHA's Single-Unit Approval process for a project that isn't on HUD's approved list — generally available only for projects of five or more units, with its own owner-occupancy and commercial-space requirements. Not every Eastside condo building carries FHA approval, and approval status can lapse or change. If a condo is part of your search, ask your agent or lender to check the building's FHA status before you write an offer — our condo vs. townhouse guide and HOA & condo documents guide cover what else to review before you commit.

The FHA appraisal looks at more than value

An FHA appraisal checks the home's market value like any appraisal, but it also screens for HUD's minimum property standards — safety, soundness, and security issues the appraiser is required to flag, such as missing handrails, exposed wiring, a non-functioning heating system, or peeling paint on a pre-1978 home (a lead-based paint concern). This matters more in parts of King County's older housing stock, where issues like knob-and-tube wiring or past moisture damage show up regularly — see our rundown of common inspection findings in Pacific Northwest homes. If the appraiser flags something, it typically has to be repaired before the loan can close, which is a different dynamic than a conventional loan, where repairs are more purely a negotiation between buyer and seller.

How FHA compares to your other options

  • Vs. conventional: FHA usually wins on credit-score flexibility and sometimes on upfront costs for lower-credit borrowers, but conventional mortgage insurance (PMI) cancels once you reach 20% equity, while FHA's typically doesn't. If your credit is strong enough to qualify conventional with a competitive PMI rate, run the long-term numbers before defaulting to FHA.
  • Vs. VA: If you're an eligible veteran or service member, VA financing usually beats FHA outright — no down payment, no monthly mortgage insurance, and often no county loan limit for buyers with full entitlement. See our VA loan guide for the details.
  • Vs. jumbo: Once your loan amount would exceed $1,063,750, you're out of FHA and conventional-conforming territory and into jumbo underwriting, which typically wants a higher credit score, a larger down payment, and more cash reserves. Our jumbo loan guide walks through what changes.

Getting pre-approved and ready to offer

Whichever loan type fits, get a real pre-approval — not just a quick pre-qualification — before you start touring homes seriously. Our mortgage pre-approval guide covers the documents lenders want and how long a pre-approval letter holds up. And because King County's Eastside market still moves fast on well-priced listings, a clean, FHA-ready offer — one where your lender has already confirmed the loan amount, condo approval status if relevant, and seller-concession math — puts you in a stronger position than scrambling to sort out financing details after your offer is already accepted.
