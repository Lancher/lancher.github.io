Buying Waterfront Property
on Lake Washington or Lake Sammamish

Shi Hao Liu
Licensed WA Real Estate Broker ・ Kelly Right Real Estate ・ Updated November 17, 2026

Waterfront property on Lake Washington or Lake Sammamish comes with a layer of rules that ordinary Eastside homes don't have: you likely don't own the lakebed itself, docks and bulkheads need permits from more than one agency, and shoreline setbacks vary by city. None of that should scare you off a waterfront purchase — it just means due diligence looks different. Here's what to check before you write an offer.

You probably don't own the lakebed

This surprises a lot of first-time waterfront buyers. Washington's constitution asserts state ownership of the beds and shores of navigable waters, and both Lake Washington and Lake Sammamish are treated as navigable lakes. The Department of Natural Resources manages millions of acres of this kind of state-owned aquatic land across Washington, including these two lakes. Unlike some other states, Washington did not grant adjoining upland owners automatic "riparian" rights to build over the water at statehood — instead, lakefront owners generally received a statutory preference right tied to the shorelands next to their lot.

In practice, this means your deed conveys the dry land up to the lake's ordinary high water mark, plus the right to reasonable use of the water in front of your property (docks, swimming, boat access), but not ownership of the submerged land the way you'd own a backyard. Lake Washington's shorelines also have their own specific statutory history tied to the lake's 1916 lowering. The upshot for a buyer: a title company review of the specific parcel is the only reliable way to confirm what you're actually acquiring, especially on an older lot where a dock, bulkhead, or buoy may predate current rules.

Docks, piers, and buoys: expect more than one permit

Building, replacing, or substantially repairing a dock on either lake typically runs through several layers of review rather than a single city permit:

  • Local shoreline review. Every lakefront parcel falls under a city or county Shoreline Master Program, adopted locally and approved by the Washington Department of Ecology under the state Shoreline Management Act. Larger projects need a Substantial Development Permit; smaller ones may qualify for an exemption, but the underlying shoreline development standards still apply either way.
  • State Hydraulic Project Approval (HPA). Any project that could affect the bed or flow of the lake — new or replacement docks, piers, buoys, bulkheads — needs an HPA from the Washington Department of Fish and Wildlife, applied for through WDFW's online permitting system. Expect conditions around construction timing and materials where salmon or other protected fish are present.
  • Federal review. Depending on the structure and location, the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers may also need to authorize the work under its own permit program for navigable waters.

Washington's Joint Aquatic Resources Permit Application (JARPA) lets applicants apply for several of these state and federal permits through one combined form, which helps, but it doesn't eliminate the underlying review. If you're buying a home with an existing dock, ask the seller for any permits on file — a dock built or substantially modified without the right approvals can become your problem to resolve, not theirs, once you own the lot.

Shoreline setbacks vary by city — there's no single number

Because each city and county writes its own Shoreline Master Program, setback and buffer distances from the water differ between, for example, unincorporated King County, Bellevue, Kirkland, and Sammamish — and can differ further within the same jurisdiction depending on the shoreline's environment designation (urban, residential, conservancy, and so on). An older home may sit well inside a current setback as a legally nonconforming structure, which matters enormously if you're planning a remodel, addition, or teardown-rebuild. Before you get attached to a waterfront listing's expansion potential, have your agent or a land-use consultant confirm the current setback and any nonconforming allowances directly with that city or county's shoreline administrator — don't rely on what a neighboring property was allowed to build, since rules and interpretations do change over time.

Bulkheads and shoreline armoring

Many older Lake Washington and Lake Sammamish lots have a bulkhead (a retaining wall at the water's edge) built decades ago. If you ever plan to replace one, know that Washington's hydraulic code has shifted toward favoring "soft shore" erosion control — things like beach nourishment, regrading, and vegetation — over traditional hard concrete or rock bulkheads, and replacing a bulkhead can require an HPA along with consideration of those softer alternatives. An existing, legally built bulkhead generally isn't something a new owner has to tear out, but assume that any future work on it will mean a fresh, and possibly more involved, permitting process than it would have been when it was first built.

Financing a waterfront purchase

A few practical financing wrinkles come with the territory:

  • Jumbo loans are common. King County's 2026 conforming loan limit is well below what most true waterfront parcels sell for, so plan on jumbo underwriting — see our jumbo loan guide for how that process differs from a conforming loan.
  • Appraisals can take longer. True waterfront comparables are scarce, so appraisers sometimes need more time, or adjust more heavily, than on a typical Eastside home.
  • Flood zone status matters. Lenders will check whether structures on the lot fall inside a FEMA-mapped flood zone, which affects insurance requirements; see our flood zone insurance guide for how that works in Washington.

Get pre-approved early and tell your lender up front that you're shopping waterfront — see our mortgage pre-approval guide for the basics that still apply.

Inspection add-ons specific to waterfront lots

A standard home inspection doesn't typically cover the dock, bulkhead, or bank stability in detail, so waterfront buyers often add:

  • A dock and bulkhead condition assessment from a marine contractor, separate from your general home inspector.
  • A geotechnical evaluation on sloped or bluff lots where bank stability or erosion is a concern.
  • A sewer scope, since many older lakefront homes predate current sewer connections — our sewer scope inspection guide covers why this matters.

Our broader guide to common inspection findings in Pacific Northwest homes covers issues that show up on waterfront and non-waterfront homes alike, like moisture intrusion and aging systems.

Check the seller's disclosures closely

Washington's Form 17 seller disclosure asks about known shoreline, flood, and environmental issues, and it's worth reading line by line on a waterfront purchase rather than skimming it. Our Form 17 guide walks through what sellers are required to disclose and where the gaps tend to be.

Where this fits in your buying process

True lakefront inventory on the Eastside is thin, which means desirable listings in places like Medina, Mercer Island, and lakefront pockets of Kirkland can draw multiple offers quickly.
