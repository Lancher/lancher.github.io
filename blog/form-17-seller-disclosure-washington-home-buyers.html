Form 17 Seller Disclosure
What Washington Home Buyers Need to Know

Shi Hao Liu
Licensed WA Real Estate Broker ・ Kelly Right Real Estate ・ Updated October 31, 2026

"Form 17" is the seller disclosure statement Washington law requires for most home sales — a questionnaire where the seller discloses what they actually know about the property's title, structure, systems, and environmental condition. Under RCW 64.06, the seller generally must deliver it within five business days of mutual acceptance, and you then get three business days to review it and walk away for any reason. It's a legal protection, not a substitute for an inspection — here's how it actually works.

What Form 17 actually is (and isn't)

Form 17 is a standardized form, published through the state's real estate forms system and used statewide, that asks the seller a long list of yes/no/don't-know questions grouped into categories: title, water, sewer/on-site systems, structural, systems and fixtures, homeowners' association, and environmental. The seller fills it out based on their personal knowledge of the home — not based on a professional inspection, a survey, or anything they'd have to go investigate to answer.

That distinction matters. A seller who's never had the roof inspected can honestly answer "don't know" to a roof-age question, even if the roof is actually failing. Form 17 tells you what the seller is willing to say they know; it doesn't tell you the objective condition of the home. That's exactly why it complements, rather than replaces, your own home inspection contingency — see our guide to common inspection findings in Pacific Northwest homes for the kinds of issues inspectors actually catch that a disclosure form might miss entirely.

The form groups its questions into a handful of sections, each worth reading on its own terms:

  • Title. Easements, boundary disputes, shared driveways, or encroachments the seller knows about.
  • Water, sewer & on-site systems. Whether the home is on a public sewer or a septic system, the water source, and known problems with either — see our septic & well inspection guide if the property isn't on city utilities.
  • Structural. Foundation, roof, additions, and known settling or water intrusion.
  • Systems & fixtures. Electrical, plumbing, heating, and major appliances included in the sale.
  • Homeowners' association. Whether the property is subject to HOA dues or governing documents.
  • Environmental. Contamination, underground storage tanks, hazardous materials, and similar issues — covered in its own section below.

The two deadlines that matter: 5 days to deliver, 3 days to rescind

Under RCW 64.06, the seller generally must deliver the completed Form 17 to you no later than five business days after mutual acceptance of your purchase and sale agreement, unless you and the seller agree in writing to a different deadline. Once you actually receive it, the clock flips to you: you have three business days to review it and, if you don't like what you see, rescind the agreement by delivering written notice to the seller — no reason required, and no negotiation needed.

If your closing date happens to fall inside that three-day window, the closing is pushed back until the rescission period runs out. That's a meaningful protection, but it only works if you actually read the form the moment it arrives rather than letting it sit in your inbox during a tight closing timeline.

Why "don't know" isn't a loophole — but it isn't nothing, either

Under RCW 64.06, a seller generally isn't liable for an error, inaccuracy, or omission on Form 17 unless they had actual knowledge of the problem when they signed it. That's a real legal standard, and it cuts both ways. A seller who answers honestly based on what they know is protected even if the answer turns out to be wrong — but a seller who did know about a leaking roof, a failed septic system, or unpermitted work and checked "no" or "don't know" anyway can be exposed to liability for misrepresentation. The practical lesson for a buyer: treat every "don't know" as a flag worth investigating yourself, not as proof there's nothing there, and don't treat a clean-looking Form 17 as a reason to skip or shorten your inspection.

The one section you generally can't waive: environmental

Buyers in competitive markets sometimes waive their right to receive Form 17 altogether to make an offer more attractive — it's legal, and it happens. But there's a carve-out that doesn't bend: a buyer cannot waive receiving any "yes" answer in the environmental section, which covers things like known soil or groundwater contamination, underground storage tanks, and other hazardous conditions. If a seller discloses a "yes" there, you're entitled to see it regardless of any waiver you signed. Given how expensive environmental remediation can be, that's one disclosure worth reading carefully even if you waived everything else to win the offer.

When there's no Form 17 at all

Not every sale requires one. The statute exempts several categories of transfers, most notably foreclosures and deeds-in-lieu of foreclosure, and new construction sold by a registered builder to its first occupant-buyer (new-construction sellers still fill out a shorter version of the form, skipping the structural questions that don't apply to a home no one has ever lived in). If you're buying a foreclosure, a short sale, or brand-new construction, don't assume a missing Form 17 means something's being hidden — it may simply not be required for that type of sale. It's still worth asking why, and leaning more heavily on your own inspection and the builder's warranty in the new-construction case. See our guide on buying new construction in Washington for how that process differs.

Condos play by a different form

Form 17 is written for single-family residential property. If you're buying a condo, the HOA-related questions on Form 17 look different, and the document that really matters is the association's resale certificate — financials, reserves, pending litigation, and governing documents — which comes with its own separate disclosure and cancellation rights under state condo law. We cover that timeline in detail in HOA & condo documents for Washington buyers, and in condo vs. townhouse if you're still deciding between the two.

Why this matters more in a fast Eastside market

In a competitive multiple-offer situation on the Eastside, buyers are sometimes pressured to waive contingencies and disclosures to look more attractive on paper. Waiving your right to receive Form 17 is one of the levers some buyers pull — but it's worth knowing exactly what you're giving up before you do. A seller's disclosure costs you nothing to read and can surface a known issue (a past water intrusion, an unpermitted addition, a title cloud) well before you're locked into an accepted offer. If you're weighing whether to waive it alongside other terms like an escalation clause, think of Form 17 as one of the cheapest forms of protection available to you in the whole transaction — rarely the term worth giving up first.

What to do when something on the form raises a flag

  • Don't treat "don't know" as "no." Follow up with your agent or directly ask the listing agent for more detail before you waive anything based on the answer.
  • Cross-check against the inspection. If the form says no known roof issues but your inspector finds obvious shingle damage, that gap is worth raising during negotiation, not ignoring.
  • Keep your own copy. If a dispute ever comes up after closing, the signed disclosure statement — and the date you received it — is part of the record.
  • Don't rely on it for anything that needs independent verification. Flood zone status, square footage, and permit history are all better confirmed through FEMA's own maps, a survey, or the county's permit records than through a seller's recollection.
