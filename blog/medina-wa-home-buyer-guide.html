Buying a Home in Medina, WA
A 2026 Buyer's Guide

Shi Hao Liu
Licensed WA Real Estate Broker ・ Kelly Right Real Estate ・ Updated November 13, 2026

Medina is a roughly 1.4-square-mile city of just under 3,000 people on the east shore of Lake Washington, directly across the Evergreen Point Floating Bridge from Seattle. It's one of the smallest and most private housing markets on the Eastside — a single ZIP code (98039), one school district, and a handful of home sales in any given month, which is exactly why its headline price figures swing so wildly from one real estate site to the next. Here's what actually matters if you're considering a purchase here.

Where Medina fits

Medina sits on a narrow strip of Lake Washington shoreline between Bellevue and the lake itself, reached from Seattle by State Route 520 across the Evergreen Point Floating Bridge, which lands directly on the city's western edge. The city was platted in 1914 and incorporated in 1955, in large part so residents could control their own zoning rather than be annexed into a larger neighbor. That history still shapes the place: Medina has no commercial strip, no multifamily zoning to speak of, and a deliberately low-key, low-density character that residents and the city council have continued to protect — in 2025, for example, the council opted not to adopt a statewide Complete Streets ordinance in part to preserve the city's existing semi-rural streetscape, even though it meant forgoing a pool of state grant funding tied to that program.

The Points: Medina's closest neighbors

Medina is often grouped informally with three smaller incorporated cities nearby — Hunts Point, Yarrow Point, and Clyde Hill — collectively known as "the Points." Each is its own city with its own council, but all four sit on or near the Evergreen Point Bridge corridor, share Bellevue School District, and have a similar small-lot, high-privacy character. If you're comparing Medina against its closest substitutes, Clyde Hill and Yarrow Point are usually the two worth cross-shopping; a local pedestrian and bike route, the Points Loop Trail, connects the four communities for residents who want to explore on foot.

One school district, not three

Unlike Newcastle, where three different districts split one small city, Medina is served entirely by Bellevue School District. Addresses in the city generally feed Medina Elementary School and Chinook Middle School ahead of Bellevue High School. As with any Eastside purchase, confirm the specific assigned schools for an address directly with the district's boundary tool before you write an offer — attendance zones can still shift at the edges even within a single district.

Lake Washington shoreline rules

Because Medina sits almost entirely along Lake Washington, a meaningful share of its lots fall under the city's Shoreline Master Program, which applies to land within 200 feet of the lake's ordinary high-water mark. Homes in that shoreline jurisdiction face extra permitting steps for docks, bulkheads, and any work close to the water, on top of the city's standard zoning code (Title 16 of the Medina Municipal Code), which sets separate development standards across its R-16, R-20, and R-30 residential districts. If you're looking at a waterfront or near-waterfront lot, ask your agent to pull the applicable zoning district and shoreline designation for that specific parcel early — it affects what you can build or rebuild long before it affects your offer price.

Overlake Golf & Country Club

Medina is home to Overlake Golf & Country Club, a private club first organized in 1925 with a course that opened in 1927 — one of the older golf clubs in the Seattle area, after a mid-century closure and 1953 reopening. It's membership-based rather than public, so it functions more as a neighborhood landmark and occasional source of golf-course-adjacent lots than as a public amenity, unlike the public courses at the Golf Club at Newcastle.

What homes cost in Medina in 2026

Pin down a single "median price" for Medina and you'll get a different number depending on the day and the source — not because anyone is wrong, but because so few homes change hands here that one or two multi-million-dollar closings can swing a monthly median by millions of dollars. Recent snapshots from Redfin and other listing-aggregator sites have reported medians ranging from roughly $3.6 million to over $6.8 million across different months in 2025 and 2026. For comparison, King County's overall median sale price (homes and condos combined) was roughly $850,000 as of September 2026, per Northwest MLS data — multiple times lower than even the low end of Medina's range. The practical takeaway: don't anchor a Medina budget to any single published "median." Pull actual closed comps on the specific street or block you're considering, ideally through your agent's MLS access, before setting a number.

What to watch for as a buyer

  • Financing almost always means jumbo. At these price points, you're well past King County's conforming loan limit. Our jumbo loan guide covers how qualifying and down payment requirements differ from a conventional loan.
  • Shoreline and tree-removal permitting take longer. Any work near the lake, or removal of significant trees, typically needs city sign-off under the Shoreline Master Program and municipal code — budget time for it if a renovation is part of your plan.
  • Privacy and security are part of the market. Several well-known residents have owned property in Medina over the years, and the city's low profile is itself part of what buyers are paying for — don't expect public open houses to be heavily marketed the way they are in more typical Eastside suburbs.
  • Thin inventory means patience and strong comps matter more than speed. With so few listings active at once, a rushed, under-researched offer is a bigger risk here than in a higher-volume market like Sammamish or Issaquah.
