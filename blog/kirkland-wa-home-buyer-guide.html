Buying a Home in Kirkland, WA
A 2026 Buyer's Guide

Shi Hao Liu
Licensed WA Real Estate Broker ・ Kelly Right Real Estate ・ Updated October 29, 2026

Kirkland pairs a walkable, waterfront downtown on Lake Washington with Google's largest Eastside campus — and it's one of the only major Eastside cities with no Link light rail station today. Zillow put the typical Kirkland home value at about $1.23 million in mid-2026, while Resideline's closed-sale data put the six-month median sold price near $1.25 million. Nearly all of the city feeds into the top-rated Lake Washington School District, and a long-planned bus rapid transit line to Bellevue won't open until around 2030. Here's what actually matters if you're considering a purchase here.

Where Kirkland fits

Kirkland sits on the west side of the Eastside, directly across Lake Washington from Seattle and bordered by Bothell and Woodinville to the north, Redmond to the east, and Bellevue to the south. It's one of King County's older and more established suburbs — the 2020 Census counted 92,175 residents, making it one of the Eastside's larger cities by population. The city spans two main ZIP codes, 98033 covering central and south Kirkland (downtown, Houghton, Rose Hill) and 98034 covering north Kirkland (Juanita, Finn Hill, Totem Lake, Kingsgate). As with any city split across ZIP codes, prices and school assignment can differ meaningfully between the two, so confirm which ZIP a specific address falls in before comparing listings.

Getting around: I-405, and no light rail — yet

Kirkland's main corridor is I-405, which runs the length of the city and connects south to Bellevue and north to Bothell. Unlike Bellevue, Redmond, and Mercer Island, Kirkland has no Link light rail station today. An extension to a South Kirkland station has been studied by Sound Transit for years but isn't funded for construction. Two transit projects are in the pipeline but still years out: King County Metro's RapidRide K Line, a bus rapid transit route connecting Kirkland and Bellevue, currently targets a 2030 launch, and Sound Transit's Stride S1 bus rapid transit line on I-405 — which would add a station near NE 85th Street in Kirkland as part of a large interchange project now under construction — is currently targeted for 2028. Both dates have slipped before, so verify current timelines with Metro and Sound Transit rather than assuming either opens on schedule. In the meantime, the Cross Kirkland Corridor, a former rail line converted into a roughly 5.75-mile paved trail through the city, is a popular walking and biking link between neighborhoods, and buses connect to the South Kirkland and Totem Lake park-and-ride lots for commuters heading into Seattle or Bellevue.

Mostly one school district

Nearly all of Kirkland is served by Lake Washington School District, one of the highest-rated districts in the state, serving roughly 31,000 students across more than 50 schools shared with Redmond and parts of Sammamish. Depending on address, Kirkland students typically feed into Juanita High School or Lake Washington High School, with some areas zoned for the district's smaller choice schools. Confirm the exact attendance zone for any specific address with the district before you buy. For how school districts compare across the broader Eastside, see our Eastside school districts guide.

Kirkland's neighborhoods

Kirkland's official neighborhood map divides the city into about a dozen areas, spanning a dense waterfront core and quieter residential hillsides:

  • Moss Bay & Market — downtown Kirkland's lakefront core, with Marina Park, the Saturday market, galleries, restaurants, and a mix of condos and older homes within walking distance of the water.
  • Norkirk — a residential neighborhood just north and east of downtown, known for its mix of older bungalows and newer infill homes close to the core.
  • Central Houghton — a lakeside neighborhood south of downtown near Carillon Point, with some of Kirkland's priciest waterfront and view properties.
  • Juanita — a larger residential area in north Kirkland anchored by Juanita Beach Park on Lake Washington, with a mix of mid-century and newer housing.
  • Finn Hill — a mostly residential, forested area in the city's northwest corner, bordering Juanita Bay.
  • North & South Rose Hill, Bridle Trails — residential neighborhoods east of downtown; Bridle Trails is known for its equestrian heritage and the adjacent Bridle Trails State Park.
  • Totem Lake & Evergreen Hill (Kingsgate) — Kirkland's northernmost area, home to the redeveloped Totem Lake urban center (shops, a medical campus, and newer apartments and townhomes) and more affordable housing stock further north toward Kingsgate.

What homes cost in Kirkland in 2026

Zillow's typical home value for Kirkland ran about $1.23 million as of mid-2026, down roughly 5.3% from a year earlier, with a median sale price near $1.24 million. Resideline's closed-sale data tells a similar story: over the six months ending in August 2026, the median sold price across 779 closings was about $1.25 million, with MLS-listed sales alone running somewhat higher. For comparison, King County's countywide median sale price for homes and condos combined was $845,000 in August 2026 per NWMLS data — well below Kirkland's figures, reflecting the premium on lakefront and near-downtown property. As with any market, these are citywide snapshots that blend downtown condos with houses on the hill; ask your agent for comparables specific to the neighborhood and property type you're considering.

Google and Kirkland's economy

Kirkland's biggest recent economic shift has been the growth of Google's local workforce, anchored by its Kirkland Urban mixed-use campus downtown. Reporting based on city data put Google's Kirkland-based employee count at roughly 5,076 in 2025, up about 47% from roughly 3,449 in 2024 — making Google one of the Eastside's fastest-growing tech employers outside of Microsoft and Amazon's home cities. That growth has added to demand for housing within an easy commute of downtown Kirkland, alongside longer-standing employers in biotech and healthcare along the I-405 corridor.

What's changing in Kirkland

Two projects are reshaping the city right now. Totem Lake, once an aging strip-mall corridor, has been rebuilt over the past decade into a mixed-use urban center with retail, a hospital campus, and new multifamily housing, and further redevelopment is still underway. And on I-405, Sound Transit and WSDOT have broken ground on a large freeway interchange project at NE 85th Street that will eventually carry the Stride S1 bus rapid transit station — expect ongoing construction and related traffic impacts along that stretch of I-405 for the next several years.

What to watch for as a buyer

  • Competitive offers near downtown and the water. Walkable, lakefront-adjacent listings in Moss Bay, Houghton, and Norkirk draw strong demand and often sell quickly. Our guides on winning a bidding war and escalation clauses cover strategy and terms.
  • Which ZIP and neighborhood you're actually buying into. Price, lake proximity, and school assignment can all shift meaningfully between south Kirkland (98033) and north Kirkland (98034) — confirm both before comparing listings.
  • No light rail — plan your commute around buses and I-405. If a fast rail commute to Seattle or Bellevue matters to you, Kirkland doesn't offer it yet; weigh that against the city's other advantages before buying.
  • Older homes in established neighborhoods. Homes from the 1950s through 1980s in areas like Norkirk, Finn Hill, or Rose Hill can hide aging wiring, plumbing, or drainage issues a walkthrough won't reveal. See our home inspection contingency guide and common PNW inspection findings.
  • Construction near Totem Lake and I-405. Ongoing redevelopment and freeway interchange work mean periodic construction noise and traffic in those areas for years to come.
