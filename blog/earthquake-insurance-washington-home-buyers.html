Earthquake Insurance in Washington
What Home Buyers Need to Know

Shi Hao Liu
Licensed WA Real Estate Broker ・ Kelly Right Real Estate ・ Updated November 9, 2026

Your standard Washington homeowners policy does not cover earthquake damage — that's true no matter where in the state you buy. No law requires you to carry earthquake coverage, and most lenders don't require it either, so it's entirely your call. If you want it, you buy it separately, either as an endorsement added to your homeowners policy or as its own standalone policy, and the deductible works differently than you're probably used to: it's a percentage of your coverage limit, not a flat dollar amount. Here's how the coverage, the cost, and the actual risk on the Eastside fit together.

Why your homeowners policy already excludes this

Earthquake damage sits in the same bucket as flood damage on a standard homeowners (HO-3) policy: both are specifically excluded, along with related perils like landslide, mudflow, and earth movement or settling. This isn't a Washington-specific quirk — it's standard across the industry nationwide — but it matters more here than in most states, given the region's seismic hazards. If a quake cracks your foundation or shifts your home off its footing, a standard policy simply won't respond to that claim.

Washington also doesn't run a state-backed earthquake insurance pool the way California does with the California Earthquake Authority. Every earthquake policy sold here comes from a private insurer, which is part of why pricing and terms vary more from company to company than you might expect.

How you actually get coverage

There are two ways to add earthquake protection:

  • Endorsement on your existing homeowners policy. Many insurers let you add earthquake coverage as a rider to the policy you already have, which is often the simplest route.
  • Standalone earthquake policy. Some buyers instead purchase a separate policy specifically for earthquake risk, sometimes through a different carrier than their main homeowners insurer.

Either way, ask your insurance agent to walk through exactly what's included — building coverage, contents, and loss-of-use (additional living expenses if the home becomes unlivable) are often sold as separate line items, similar to how flood coverage splits building and contents into two purchases. See our flood insurance guide for a comparison of how that other excluded-peril coverage works.

What it costs — and how the deductible really works

Earthquake insurance pricing varies by insurer, the home's construction (wood-frame homes generally cost less to insure than older brick or unreinforced masonry structures), location, and the coverage limit you choose, so there's no single reliable "typical cost" figure to quote — get an actual quote for your specific property before you rely on an estimate.

The detail that surprises most first-time buyers is the deductible. Instead of a flat amount like the $1,000 or $2,500 deductible on your regular homeowners policy, earthquake deductibles are expressed as a percentage of your dwelling coverage limit — commonly ranging from 10% to 25%, according to Washington's insurance regulator. That means your out-of-pocket exposure scales with your home's insured value: on a home insured for $600,000 with a 15% deductible, you'd owe $90,000 before the policy pays a dollar. Run that math for your own home's insured value and the deductible options you're quoted — it changes how much the coverage is actually protecting you against.

Does your lender require it?

Generally, no. Flood insurance has a federal mandate behind it in FEMA-designated flood zones; earthquake insurance doesn't have an equivalent. No Washington state law and no federal law forces a lender to require earthquake coverage as a condition of a standard home loan, and most residential lenders don't ask for it. Loan requirements are set lender by lender and loan by loan, though, so confirm directly with your loan officer rather than assuming — it's one more thing worth asking about during the same underwriting conversation covered in our mortgage pre-approval guide.

Is this really an Eastside issue, or just a Seattle one?

It reaches the whole region. King County sits above two distinct seismic hazards. The first is the Cascadia Subduction Zone, offshore of the Pacific Northwest coast, capable of producing a magnitude 9 "megaquake" — the event often called "the Big One." Scientific estimates of the odds of a major Cascadia earthquake in the next 50 years vary by study and by which segment of the roughly 700-mile fault is examined, with published estimates generally ranging from around 15% to 40%. The second is the Seattle Fault, a shallower crustal fault that runs roughly east-west through the Puget Sound area and is capable of a damaging earthquake on its own, independent of Cascadia. Smaller local faults in the Puget Sound region add to the picture as well.

What that means practically: shaking intensity in a major quake depends heavily on local soil conditions and distance from the specific fault involved, not simply on how far a home sits from downtown Seattle. An Eastside home isn't automatically safer just because it's inland from the coast, and it isn't automatically riskier just because it's closer to the Seattle Fault's path — the honest answer is that risk varies by specific location and soil type across the whole region, including Redmond, Bellevue, and Kirkland.

Older homes: the retrofit question worth asking

Seismic building code requirements have gotten substantially stronger over the decades, and many older Puget Sound-area homes — particularly those built before roughly 1980 — were constructed without features modern code requires, such as foundation bolting (anchoring the wood framing to the concrete foundation) and bracing for "cripple walls" (the short stud walls between the foundation and the first floor, common in homes with a crawl space). Neither of these is something you'll necessarily see from the curb or even notice in a quick walkthrough, and they're not the kind of thing most listing photos show off. If you're considering an older home, this is a good question for your home inspector, and relevant background in our common home inspection findings guide. Retrofitting an older home with these features is a separate project cost worth factoring in if you're weighing whether to also buy earthquake coverage.

What Form 17 does — and doesn't — tell you

Washington's seller disclosure statement, required under RCW 64.06 and commonly called Form 17, doesn't have a dedicated earthquake question the way it specifically asks about flood zones. It does, however, ask sellers to disclose known defects or problems with the foundation and structure, which is exactly where seismic retrofit gaps and quake-related damage tend to surface. Like every answer on Form 17, it only reflects what the seller actually knows — not an independent structural inspection — so it's a starting point, not a substitute for having your own inspector look at the foundation and crawl space directly.
