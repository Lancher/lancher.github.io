Jumbo Loans in King County
What Home Buyers Need to Know

Shi Hao Liu
Licensed WA Real Estate Broker ・ Kelly Right Real Estate ・ Updated November 3, 2026

In 2026, the conforming loan limit for a one-unit home in King, Pierce, and Snohomish counties is $1,063,750 — any conventional loan above that amount is a jumbo loan. That's well above the $832,750 baseline that applies to most other Washington counties, because federal regulators treat these three counties as a high-cost area based on local home values. But it's still below the median price in several Eastside cities, which means buyers putting down less than 20% on an otherwise ordinary home can cross into jumbo territory without realizing it. Here's how the limit works, how FHA and VA loans compare, and what changes once you're borrowing jumbo.

What makes a loan "jumbo" in the first place

A loan is jumbo whenever it exceeds the conforming loan limit set each year by the Federal Housing Finance Agency (FHFA) for the county where the home sits. "Conforming" means the loan is small enough that Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac can buy it from the lender on the secondary market, which keeps conforming loans standardized and generally cheaper to originate. Jumbo loans can't be sold to Fannie or Freddie, so the lender making the loan either keeps it on its own books or sells it through a different channel — and sets its own underwriting rules as a result, rather than following Fannie/Freddie guidelines.

King County's 2026 loan limit: $1,063,750

FHFA sets a national baseline conforming loan limit every year — $832,750 for a one-unit home in 2026 — and then raises it in counties where local home values run high enough to qualify as a "high-cost area." King, Pierce, and Snohomish counties all carry the same elevated 2026 limit: $1,063,750 for a one-unit property. That's about $231,000 more borrowing room than the baseline limit that applies to the rest of Washington. The nationwide ceiling for high-cost areas in 2026 is $1,249,125 (150% of the baseline), but King County's actual limit sits below that ceiling — it's set by the county's own home values, not the maximum FHFA allows.

FHA buyers get the same higher limit

FHA loans follow their own limit, set independently by HUD, but it moves together with the conforming limit in high-cost counties. For 2026, the FHA loan limit for a one-unit home in King, Pierce, and Snohomish counties also runs at $1,063,750 — matching the conventional conforming limit. That matters for buyers using a low-down-payment FHA loan on a higher-priced Eastside home, since it means FHA financing stays available well above what FHA buyers in most of the country could borrow.

VA loans: no limit for most veterans

VA loans work differently. Since January 1, 2020, under the Blue Water Navy Vietnam Veterans Act, veterans and service members with full VA loan entitlement have had no VA-imposed loan limit at all — the actual amount they can borrow is set by the lender's own underwriting and the home's appraised value, not a county cap. Veterans with only partial entitlement remaining, typically because they still have an existing VA loan, are subject to the local county limit instead. If you're a veteran or active-duty buyer, ask your lender to confirm your entitlement status before assuming either rule applies to you.

How jumbo underwriting is actually different

Because jumbo loans aren't sold to Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac, each lender writes its own rulebook, and requirements vary more than they do for conforming loans. A few patterns show up across most jumbo programs:

  • Higher credit score. Many jumbo lenders look for a score of 700 or above, with the best pricing often reserved for borrowers at 740 or higher.
  • Larger down payment. Jumbo down payments commonly run 10-20% or more, though some lenders offer lower down payment options to borrowers with strong credit and reserves.
  • Cash reserves. Jumbo lenders frequently want to see several months — sometimes 6 to 12 — of mortgage payments (principal, interest, taxes, and insurance) sitting in reserve after closing, on top of your down payment and closing costs.
  • Tighter debt-to-income ratio. Jumbo underwriting tends to be less flexible on DTI than the automated underwriting many conforming loans get approved through.

Exact thresholds vary a lot by lender, so these are typical ranges, not fixed rules — get quotes from more than one lender if you expect to need jumbo financing.

Why "ordinary" Eastside purchases can turn into jumbo loans

King County's overall median sale price has generally stayed under the conforming limit through 2026. But several Eastside cities run well above it — for example, Zillow put Bellevue's typical home value at roughly $1.47 million as of June 30, 2026, with a median sale price closer to $1.38 million (see our full Bellevue buyer's guide). On a home in that range, even a buyer making a healthy down payment can end up borrowing more than $1,063,750 once you subtract a 10% or 15% down payment from the purchase price — which is why jumbo financing comes up often on Eastside transactions even though the properties themselves aren't unusual by local standards.

The loan limit applies to your loan amount, not your purchase price. A buyer putting 20% down on a $1.2 million home is borrowing $960,000 — under the limit, so no jumbo loan needed. A buyer putting 10% down on that same home is borrowing $1.08 million — just over the limit, which triggers jumbo underwriting.

Ways buyers structure around the jumbo threshold

If staying under the conforming limit would meaningfully simplify your financing, a few options are worth discussing with your loan officer:

  • A larger down payment to bring the loan amount at or under $1,063,750, even if it means borrowing less than you're approved for.
  • A "piggyback" or 80-10-10 structure, where a conforming first mortgage is paired with a separate, smaller second loan (often a HELOC) rather than taking out one large jumbo loan.
  • Comparing jumbo and piggyback quotes side by side — rates and fees between the two vary by lender and by market conditions, so the cheaper path isn't the same in every case.

Getting pre-approved before you compete for a listing

Whether you end up conforming, FHA, VA, or jumbo, get a real pre-approval — not just a pre-qualification — before you tour homes seriously.
