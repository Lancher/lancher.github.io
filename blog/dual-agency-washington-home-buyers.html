Dual Agency in Washington
What Home Buyers Need to Know

Shi Hao Liu
Licensed WA Real Estate Broker ・ Kelly Right Real Estate ・ Updated November 25, 2026

Yes — dual agency is legal in Washington. State law calls it limited dual agency, and it happens whenever one broker, or two different agents at the same brokerage firm, end up representing both the buyer and the seller in the same sale. It only happens with your written consent, and once it does, your agent legally can't push for terms that favor the seller over you — or you over the seller. Here's how it actually works on an Eastside transaction, where it most often comes up, and when you should say no and insist on your own, undivided representation.

What "limited dual agency" means

Under Washington's real estate agency law, RCW 18.86, a broker who works with a buyer is presumed to be that buyer's agent — not a helper for the seller's side — unless everyone agrees otherwise in writing. Dual agency is the exception: it happens when a single broker, or a firm's managing broker overseeing two agents at the same company, ends up tied to both the buyer and the seller in one deal. The law requires this to be limited — a dual agent owes reduced duties to each side compared to representing just one party, and can't advocate for terms that favor one principal over the other. Washington rewrote and simplified the governing consumer pamphlet, now titled "Real Estate Brokerage in Washington," effective January 1, 2024, specifically so buyers and sellers could actually understand this distinction before signing anything.

The two ways it happens

  • Classic dual agency: One broker represents the seller (the listing) and also represents you, the buyer, on that same home. This is most common with a listing agent who offers to "also help you" buy the home they have listed, or at a small open house where the host agent writes your offer directly.
  • Same-firm, different agents (often called "designated agency"): Your buyer's agent and the seller's listing agent work for the same brokerage company, even though you've never met the other agent and each of you only deals with your own. Washington's law treats the firm's managing broker as a limited dual agent in this situation too, because the firm itself is representing both sides of the transaction even though two different individuals are doing the day-to-day work.

The second scenario is far more common than people realize on the Eastside, where a handful of large regional brokerages list a significant share of inventory. You can ask your agent directly — before you write an offer — whether their firm holds the listing on a home you're interested in.

What a dual agent can and can't do for you

A limited dual agent is required to treat both sides fairly rather than champion either one. In practice that means they generally:

  • Can't tell you the seller's confidential bottom line, and can't tell the seller your confidential maximum offer or walk-away point — that confidentiality obligation continues even after the transaction or the agency relationship ends.
  • Can't advise you on offer strategy the way an agent representing only you could, since any advice that helps you negotiate against the seller would conflict with the duties owed to the seller too.
  • Must still disclose known material facts about the property to both sides and treat both parties honestly.

None of that makes a dual agent dishonest — it makes them structurally limited. The advocacy, strategy, and negotiating leverage you'd get from an agent who represents only you simply isn't available once that same person (or firm) also answers to the seller.

Why this matters more in a competitive offer

On a well-priced Eastside listing that draws multiple offers, the value of a buyer's agent is concentrated almost entirely in advocacy: how to structure an escalation clause, what to waive and what to keep in a home inspection contingency, and how hard to push on price. See our guide on winning a bidding war for the mechanics. A limited dual agent is restricted from giving you that kind of pointed strategic advice precisely when it matters most, because the same advice would work against the seller they also represent. If you're going into a multiple-offer situation, an agent with no tie at all to the listing side is in a much stronger position to fight only for you.

Your written consent is required — and you can say no

Dual agency can't be sprung on you. RCW 18.86 requires the written consent of every party before a limited dual agency arises, and that consent is documented in the written services agreement a broker is already required to have with you — the same agreement covered in our Buyer Brokerage Agreement guide. Compensation terms are part of that same written agreement, consistent with the disclosure requirements that followed the 2024 NAR settlement and that we cover in who pays the buyer's agent in Washington.

If a listing agent offers to also represent you, or your agent discloses that their firm holds the listing on a home you want to make an offer on, you are free to decline. You can ask for a different agent at the firm with a formal "designated agency" split (two individual agents, each solely representing one side, though the firm's managing broker remains a limited dual agent under the statute), or you can simply walk your business to a broker with no connection whatsoever to the seller's side. Either is a reasonable, common request — no listing agent should pressure you into dual representation just because it's convenient for them.

A related trap: the builder's on-site agent

A close cousin of this issue shows up with new construction. The friendly agent staffing a builder's sales office works for — and is paid by — the builder, not you, and typically isn't a dual agent at all; they're simply the seller's agent the whole time, whether or not you sign anything. Our new construction buyer's agent guide covers why bringing your own agent to a builder sales office matters just as much as avoiding dual agency on a resale home.

How to keep full, undivided advocacy

The simplest way to avoid every version of this problem is to work with a broker who represents buyers only, confirm up front that their firm doesn't hold the listing on homes you're seriously considering, and put that understanding in your written services agreement.
